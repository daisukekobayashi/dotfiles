# Mathematics learning guidance

Use the definition, derivation, calculation, model, or statistical analysis in
the current work. Include statistics and probability when relevant. Choose the
reasoning that matters to the user's question rather than a syllabus of topics.

## Establish the statement and assumptions

Make the objects, notation, domain, and claim precise enough to reason about.
Distinguish a definition from a theorem, an assumption from a conclusion, and an
exact statement from an approximation. Identify the conditions that justify the
step or inference the user is trying to understand.

Use a small case, diagram, or intuitive explanation to make the structure
accessible. Connect that intuition back to the formal statement; avoid presenting
a useful analogy as a proof or making symbolic fluency a prerequisite for every
conceptual discussion.

## Match the check to the claim

- For a derivation or proof, examine why each consequential step follows, where
  assumptions enter, and whether the conclusion covers the claimed domain.
- For a general assertion, a counterexample can expose a missing condition.
  Agreement on selected examples does not establish the general statement.
- For a numerical result, inspect units, scale, limiting cases, approximation
  error, and numerical stability when they affect the conclusion.
- For a simulation, separate what was observed under the chosen parameters from
  a mathematical guarantee or a claim about the real world.

Choose a check that addresses the uncertain step. Repeating the same calculation
with the same assumptions may leave that uncertainty unresolved.

## Interpret statistical evidence

Identify the target quantity, population, observational unit, and how data were
selected or measured. Examine the assumptions that connect the data to the
inference, including dependence, missingness, and model choice where relevant.

Interpret an estimate alongside its uncertainty and practical meaning. Distinguish
description, prediction, and causal claims. A formula or successful model fit does
not establish that its assumptions hold for the data; inspect diagnostics,
alternative explanations, or sensitivity to plausible changes.

## Transfer the reasoning

When useful, change an assumption, domain, scale, or data-generating condition.
Invite the user to identify which steps remain justified and which need revision.
Recognizing that an inference is no longer supported is a useful outcome, even
when there is not enough information to produce a replacement answer.
