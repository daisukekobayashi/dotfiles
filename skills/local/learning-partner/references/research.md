# Research learning guidance

Use the paper, report, analysis, or research question under discussion. This
guidance can accompany any subject-specific reference. Focus on how a claim is
supported and where it applies rather than recalling a summary of the conclusions.

## Trace a claim to its evidence

Identify the research question, consequential claim, and assumptions needed to
interpret it. Trace the claim to the relevant methods, data, argument, figure,
table, or source passage. Separate the authors' interpretation from the reported
evidence and from the user's or AI's inference.

Use the available source precisely. If only an abstract, excerpt, secondary
summary, or partial result is available, make that boundary clear. Do not invent
methods, findings, quotations, or citations to fill missing material.

## Evaluate the method in context

Match the evaluation to the kind of work. A theoretical claim may need a proof;
an empirical claim may need scrutiny of sampling, measurement, comparisons, and
analysis; an interpretive claim may depend on the selection and reading of its
source material. Do not impose one field's preferred method on every study.

Examine the choices that could materially change the conclusion: operational
definitions, sample or case selection, baselines, controls, uncertainty, excluded
observations, or alternative explanations. Distinguish an association, a causal
claim, and a claim about applicability beyond the studied setting.

Evidence that a method can work in the reported setting may leave its comparative
advantage or practical usefulness open. Publication status and confident wording
do not replace inspection of the supporting argument.

## Choose a discriminating question or check

Invite the user to explain which evidence supports the claim, identify a missing
assumption, compare an alternative explanation, or predict a result that would
weaken the conclusion. When useful, inspect a source passage, calculation, or
small reproduction that can distinguish the competing accounts.

Keep conclusions proportional to what was checked. Reproducing one result does
not establish every claim in a paper; an unsuccessful reproduction needs its
conditions examined before drawing a conclusion about the original work.

## Transfer the interpretation

When useful, vary the population, setting, measurement, assumptions, or intended
application. Invite the user to identify what evidence carries over and what
would require a new argument or observation. End with the claim's scope and a
meaningful unresolved question when the available evidence cannot settle it.
